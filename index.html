About

What happens to a people after the land is lost?

I am a PhD candidate in Political Science at Northwestern University. My dissertation, Losing Land, Not Its People, develops the concept of territorial mourning — how displaced populations and the societies around them remember, grieve, and politically process the loss of homeland territory.

I work comparatively and historically across three cases — the Sudetenland expulsions, ceded Finnish Karelia, and post-2023 Nagorno-Karabakh — combining archival research and fieldwork with original survey experiments. Alongside the dissertation, I study conflict attitudes in pre-2022 Ukraine and work as an analyst on transatlantic politics.

Dissertation Research

Losing Land, Not Its People

Three cases, separated by geography and decades, linked by the same question: what happens to a people's sense of home when the border moves without them? Each case pairs archival and field research with an original survey experiment.

Czechoslovakia · 1938

The Sudetenland

The annexation of the Sudetenland and the postwar expulsion of ethnic Germans left behind a landscape of contested memory. This strand of the project traces how that loss has been archived, organized, and politicized across generations — from state resettlement records of the late 1930s to the discourse of present-day Sudeten German associations.

Methods: archival research in Czech and German state records; a social-media discourse pipeline (Facebook, YouTube) tracking contemporary Sudeten German organizing around the 2026 Brno sjezd; comparative-historical analysis of displacement bureaucracy.

Finland · 1940/1944

Karelia

Ceded to the Soviet Union at the end of the Continuation War, Finnish Karelia produced one of Europe's largest wartime resettlements — and, decades later, a resilient institutional memory. New Valamo Monastery, evacuated from Ladoga Karelia in 1940, is one such institution: a community that carried its identity across a new border and rebuilt.

Methods: archival and field research with Karjalan Liitto, the Karelian resettlers' association, including translation of its 2027–2029 strategic planning document; interviews and site visits across former and present Karelian communities.

Armenia · 2023

Nagorno-Karabakh

The most recent displacement in the project. Following the 2023 exodus of the Nagorno-Karabakh Armenian population, this strand tests how territorial-loss salience and rootedness shape attitudes toward return — through a CRRC-Armenia CATI survey (n = 759, general population and displaced respondents) pre-registered on OSF.

Methods: pre-registered survey experiment (H1–H3); fieldwork and site documentation in Nagorno-Karabakh prior to 2023; data-quality auditing of interviewer-level response patterns.

Academic Publications

Current pipeline

  • Working paper

    Others Might Return but I Will Not

    A paired self-other survey experiment (n = 1,048 Ukrainian refugees, Czech Republic) documenting a persistent self-other gap in expectations of permanent return, with a self-other forecasting framework and a conjoint-based test of gap structure across 21 attribute levels.

  • In revision

    Proximity, Permeability, and the Possibility of Violence

    Border Regime Distances and Conflict Attitudes in Pre-2022 Ukraine. With Aaron Erlich and Calvin Garner. Revised toward a contested risk-avoidance vs. hardening framework, targeting Journal of Peace Research.

  • Working paper

    Territorial Loss Salience and Return Intentions in Armenia

    A pre-registered CRRC-Armenia survey experiment (n = 759) testing how territorial-loss salience and rootedness shape willingness to reclaim lost territory. Abstract accepted at MAPOR; full draft in progress.
